Eternal Flight
A tissue donation service for wild vultures

Become part of something that flies.

We take a tiny sample of muscle from your arm and grow it in a lab until there is enough to feed wild vultures. We then send it to feeding stations on four continents. Each one is close to a place where vultures breed or spend the winter, and where they can no longer find enough food on their own.

Why vultures need help

Vultures live on what we leave behind. These days we leave them almost nothing.

Part 1 of 3

A medicine that worked too well

In the 1990s, vets across South Asia began giving cattle diclofenac, a cheap anti-inflammatory drug. When the cattle died their bodies were left in the open, as they always had been, and vultures ate them.

The drug was harmless to cows and fatal to vultures. It destroyed their kidneys within days. By the time scientists traced the cause, some species had lost almost all of their population.

Oaks, J.L. et al. (2004). Diclofenac residues as the cause of vulture population decline in Pakistan. Nature, 427, 630–633.

Part 2 of 3

Nothing left to find

Vultures do not hunt. They eat what has already died. In much of the modern world dead livestock are collected and destroyed, roadkill is cleared within hours, and fields are kept clean. Europe tightened its rules on carcass disposal after the BSE crisis, and countries from Spain to Korea followed similar paths.

So in many places vultures are now fed by people. Conservation groups, farmers and volunteers run feeding sites, sometimes called vulture restaurants, in the mountains of Spain, the plains of Nepal and the snowy fields of Korea. When the meat runs out, the birds go hungry.

Several vulture species are now listed as threatened or critically endangered on the IUCN Red List.

Part 3 of 3

What we put in the food

Even the food that people put out for them can be dangerous. Lead fragments from hunting ammunition, leftover veterinary drugs and poisoned bait set for other animals still turn up in the carcasses vultures eat.

A vulture has no way of knowing where its meal came from or what is inside it. It simply eats what it finds.

Why you

You already have exactly what they need.

The food we grow from your muscle is clean, and we can trace every piece of it back to you. It comes from a living person who chose to give it. No animal has to die to make it, and you don't have to wait until the end of your own life to help.

  1. Clean food they can trust

    Every batch is screened for painkillers, antibiotics and lead before it leaves the lab. Nobody can promise that about a dead animal left out in a field.

  2. Help now, while you are alive

    Most of us will be buried or cremated, and nothing will come of our bodies. You can do something different today. Half a gram of your muscle is enough to grow food for many meals.

  3. A relationship you can follow

    We tell you which station received your tissue, the day it was served and which batch it was part of. Your name is also carved into a stone wall next to the feeding ground.

  4. A part of you that flies

    When a vulture eats, the protein from its food becomes part of its own body. Some of what grew from your cells will end up in the muscles that move its wings as it flies over mountains and across borders.

Three steps

It takes about ten minutes at home.

Once the box is open there are only three things to do. The cuff handles the hard parts and you never see a needle.

Step 1

Sample

Wrap the cuff around your upper arm and press start. It numbs the skin and takes a piece of muscle about the size of a grain of rice.

Step 2

Mark

The cuff leaves a small tattoo next to where the sample was taken. You choose a feather, a pair of wings or your donor number.

Step 3

Return

Put the sealed cartridge into the return capsule and post it. It reaches our lab cold within two days.

The mark

Give once.
Wear it for life.

Every donor leaves with a small mark on the arm. It sits right next to the line where your sample came out, and it tells anyone who asks that part of you is up there somewhere. Most people get asked about it within a week.

Come back next year and you get another one. Some of our donors have a whole row now.

Questions

Things people ask us.

Does it hurt?

Less than most people expect. The cuff numbs the skin for about three minutes before it does anything else, so what you feel during sampling is mostly pressure. The tattoo module that follows feels like a fine-line tattoo on a numbed arm. The site can be sore for a day or two afterwards, much like after a vaccination.

How much of me do you actually take?

About half a gram of skeletal muscle. That's roughly the size of a grain of rice. Your body replaces it within a few weeks and you will not notice any difference in strength or movement.

Will there be a scar?

Usually a faint line about a centimetre long. The memorial mark is placed right next to it. We don't try to hide the line completely, because it is the only trace of what you gave. Most donors say they stop noticing it after a few months.

Is it still me after it's grown?

That depends on what you think makes something yours. In strict physical terms, almost none of it is. Your half gram is multiplied into about 160 kilograms, so in any single batch less than one thousandth of a percent was ever inside your body. But every one of those cells grew from yours and carries your DNA. We keep your donor number on every pack because we think that connection matters, even if it's hard to measure.

Will the vulture know my name?

No. A vulture recognises food, other vultures and the shape of the place where it is usually fed. It will never know that a person was involved. Your name lives on the wall at the station and in the notices we send you. The relationship is real, but it only runs in one direction, and we think it's important to be honest about that.

Why vultures and not some other animal?

Vultures are built to eat what others leave behind. They clean up carcasses that would otherwise rot and spread disease. That role has almost disappeared across much of the world, because people now remove dead animals before vultures can reach them. In many regions the birds that remain depend on food that humans bring. They are also among the species that have suffered most from what we put in that food.

Why not just donate meat or money?

You can, and many people already do. Feeding stations always need both. Eternal Flight is for people who want something more direct. Meat from a butcher comes from an animal you never knew. Money turns into meat you never see. Your own tissue is something you can trace from your arm to a particular field on a particular day.

Is it safe for the birds?

That's the whole reason for the screening and the testing. Every batch is checked for anti-inflammatory drugs, antibiotics and heavy metals before it leaves the lab, and nothing is released if any residue is found. Cultivated tissue is also free of the bone fragments and lead shot that sometimes end up in donated carcasses. We adjust fat and calcium levels so the food matches what a wild vulture would get from a normal meal.

Can I choose which bird eats it?

You choose the station. We can't assign an individual bird. Vultures feed in groups with their own order of who eats first, and older birds often push the young ones aside. We think it's better not to interfere with that.

Can I give more than once?

Yes, once every twelve months. Each donation opens a new cell line and leaves a new mark beside the last one. Some regular donors end up with a short row of feathers on their arm, one for each year.

What happens if I die before my line runs out?

Your cells keep growing and your batches keep being served until the line reaches its natural limit. Your notices can be sent to someone you name in advance. Your name stays on the wall either way.

Can I change my mind?

You can cancel at any point before the cartridge is posted. Once your sample reaches the lab and the line has started, we can still stop it, but we can't give anything back. Everything grown up to that point will be destroyed rather than served.
